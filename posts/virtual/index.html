How GPUs Accidentally Created Modern Intelligence
Posted on 2025-11-28 :: LLMs ML

Introduction

We are now in the AI era. Everywhere we look, AI is present, and it is considered the next big thing after the famous dot-com revolution (hopefully AI doesn’t crash like the dot-com bubble). Every week we get a new AI model from various companies claiming they are better, and by the time I write this blog, we are in the era of Claude, whose new 4.5 Opus model claims it will replace software engineers.

But when we think for a bit, all we are seeing is the AI revolution happening blazingly fast. AI used to struggle to create accurate images with detail, and it failed at multiple things like not being able to generate a hand with five fingers. And now, by this year end, we can see models like NanoBanana generating images indistinguishable from real ones, with perfect lighting and textures. Two years ago the world was writing search queries; the next year it was holding conversations with models that write code, summarize research, and imitate human reasoning.

But none of this was spontaneous. The foundations of modern AI were put in place decades ago long before transformers, long before GPT, and long before most people believed neural networks could ever scale. The real story of LLMs begins not with language, but with hardware and with chips that were never designed for intelligence at all

The Early Foundations

The story of modern AI isn't something that happened in the past few years. It started many decades ago, from a time when researchers and early scientists were building intuition about AI without any computation but with just basic systems and paper.

In 1950 the first neural network was invented. It was called the perceptron, made up of small neurons which are based on threshold logic units, and when we pair TLUs with a learning algorithm we get a neural network model. Early researchers believed they had found the fundamental unit of machine intelligence. Through the 1970s and 1980s, work continued on multilayer networks, backpropagation, convolutional structures, and the early mathematical foundations of what would eventually become deep learning.

But many researchers worked hard, had so many ideas at a massive scale, and they tried to prove that a mere computer could mimic human intelligence. People called them bluffers and said it was impossible, and all of this happened because machines were far too weak to run these ideas at meaningful scale. CPUs were slow, memory capacities were tiny, and datasets were nowhere near large enough to reveal the true power of neural networks.

Neural networks and the whole field of deep learning thrive on linear algebra, particularly matrix operations, and machines were not capable of running heavy matrix operations. The computations required for high-dimensional matrix operations were simply unrealistic for the hardware of the time.

Because of these limitations, the world slowly shifted away from neural networks. By the late 1980s and throughout the 1990s, symbolic AI, systems based on logic, rules, handcrafted knowledge, and narrow task-specific pipelines, became the dominant approach. These systems powered early chatbots, expert systems, and eventually assistants like Siri and Alexa. They behaved like AI, but they were rigid, brittle, and entirely dependent on human-written rules.

Meanwhile, neural networks never disappeared; they simply waited. The math already existed. The concepts were already understood. But the technology required to unlock their potential hadn’t arrived yet.

The Mathematical Breakthrough Waiting for Hardware

Even though neural networks were ignored for their complexity at that time, the mathematical core of modern deep learning was already understood way before. Researchers knew that if you take an input vector and pass it through a series of matrix operations and nonlinear functions, you could build systems capable of representing complex patterns. Each layer of a neural network essentially performs the transformation

y = Wx + b

where 𝑥 is the input, 𝑊 is a weight matrix, and 𝑏 is a bias. This simple equation is the basic engine that drives every modern AI system, from image classifiers to large language models.

But the mathematical simplicity hides an enormous computational cost. These transformations operate in extremely high-dimensional spaces. A single layer in a modern model might involve a 4096×4096 weight matrix, requiring tens of millions of multiplications for one forward pass. Multiply that across dozens or hundreds of layers and across millions of training examples, and the scale becomes astronomical.

This is why most of AI research was stalled during the end of the 20th century and shifted towards expert systems, and many thought the chapter was closed. This era is also called the AI winter, which lasted from 1987 to 1994.

CPUs are good for small operations, but they are not designed to perform thousands of multiplications in parallel. Neural networks, however, rely almost entirely on large-scale matrix operations workloads that are “embarrassingly parallel,” meaning they demand massive parallel computation rather than single-thread performance. Memory bandwidth was low, cache sizes were limited, and the compute units available on conventional processors were incapable of performing these huge linear algebra operations fast enough to make deep networks practical. As a result, the theory of deep learning remained far ahead of what computers were able to execute.

The Hardware Barrier and how GPU That Finally Broke It

CPUs were designed for sequential logic and branching operations, not for the huge parallel arithmetic that neural networks require. A single neural network layer is just a large matrix multiplication, and modern models involve millions of such operations across many layers. CPUs could not perform these operations fast enough to make deep learning practical.

Meanwhile during the early 2010s, we had huge improvements in the gaming industry with the introduction of the Graphics Processing Unit. It helped games achieve complex textures and effects such as smoke, water motion, and detailed lighting. Rendering a frame requires applying the same mathematical operations to thousands of pixels at once. To achieve this, GPUs were built with

  • thousands of small parallel cores
  • high memory bandwidth
  • efficient vector and matrix processing
  • SIMD/SIMT execution

This made GPUs suitable for deep learning workloads, and NVIDIA started giving access to the core hardware of GPUs for researchers via CUDA, which gave them raw parallel compute power. Suddenly, neural networks that took weeks to train on CPUs could be trained in hours on GPUs. The hardware barrier that held back deep learning for decades was finally broken.

The impact of GPUs was seen by the industry with AlexNet, a CNN developed by Alex Krizhevsky, Ilya Sutskever, and Geoffrey Hinton. Trained entirely on two NVIDIA GTX 580 GPUs, AlexNet had around 60 million parameters and used the full ImageNet dataset.The results changed AI overnight AlexNet achieved excellent results with having less errors

AlexNet proved three things very clearly:

  • Deep networks work when you have enough compute.
  • GPUs are the natural hardware for large-scale neural networks.
  • Scaling data and model size produces dramatic improvements.

The golden era of AI research began, and now that we finally had the required computation, many breakthroughs followed, from transformers to large language models.

Transformers and the Rise of LLMs

while the GPUs unlocked the compute needed for the deep learning in vision but still it face problems in language models such as LSTMs and RNNs but they processed text one token at a time, making them fundamentally slow and impossible to scale efficiently on GPUs. The hardware was finally ready, but the architecture wasn’t.That changed in 2017 with the transformer architecture introduced in Attention Is All You Need. For the first time, a model could process entire sequences in parallel using self-attention, a mechanism that lets every token directly interact with every other token.made training massively parallel, and aligned perfectly with GPU hardware. It turned language modeling into a problem dominated by matrix multiplications the exact operation GPUs excel at.

Once transformers arrived scaling become obvious.OpenAi built on this idea in 2018 it made first GPT then followed by GPT 2 in 2019 and GPT-3 in 2021 proved that sufficiently large transformers could generalize in ways no one expected. With more data, more layers, and more GPU clusters, the models began to display emergent reasoning and in-context learning.The final awe moment is GPT 3.5 trained on large data and also have touch of RLHF which made it more of artifical intelligence

This ignited a global race GPT-4, Claude, Gemini, LLaMA, Mistral, and DeepSeek all transformer based, all powered by massive GPU compute, all riding the same scaling laws.

  • Transformers provided the architecture.
  • GPUs provided the acceleration.
  • Scaling provided the breakthrough.

Together, they produced the modern era of large language models—systems that finally made the decades of deep learning research feel inevitable.

The Era We’re In and Where It Might Be Heading

Now we are in a perfect time where deep learning has scaled in a way early researchers never could have imagined. GPUs gave transformers the parallel power they needed, and that led directly to the rise of large language models. The entire field became a story of scale: bigger datasets, bigger models, bigger clusters. But we have another problem here. LLMs and all the AIs we see are just next-token predictors. Their intelligence comes from size and compute, not from understanding. They forget context, they hallucinate, and the training process itself is massively inefficient. By recent trends, it is evident that we are hitting limits of scaling, meaning no amount of scaling can improve the current AI. So we still need breakthroughs, more likely from the architecture side of AI rather than hardware. We may enter another winter, but this time it will be different. Now that companies have seen the capability of AI, the next coming years in the field of AI will be more interesting.
